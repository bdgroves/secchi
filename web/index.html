Same weather, two watersheds

Homewood and Glenbrook 5 sit 64 metres apart in latitude on opposite shores of the lake — the same line across the map, one side facing the Pacific storms and one side in the Sierra rain shadow. Both are upland hillslope stations, and both readings below are taken at the same instant, because air temperature and humidity swing far too much across a day for "latest at each site" to mean anything.

Three anchors on the lake

Oxygen appears three ways on purpose. DO is the concentration. DO sat is TEON's published saturation percentage, which is referenced to a sea-level atmosphere. DO sat (local) is the same water measured against air pressure at the lake surface — 1,898 m, about 79.5 % of sea level. The difference is not cosmetic: 81 % reads as oxygen-stressed water, while 102 % of what the water can actually hold there means photosynthesis is running ahead of respiration. Derived from TEON's own concentration and temperature, so it depends on their measurements rather than their saturation assumption.

Hand-collected sondes

Two of the five lake sondes are self-logging: they store to internal memory and are read by boat and snorkel, roughly monthly. Their records are the most complete in the network — 98.8 % against 73.6–96.8 % for the telemetered ones, because there is no radio link to drop packets. What they are not is current. These cards show the period of record we hold rather than a live reading, and what the next collection would extend from.

The lake and its outlet

Lake Tahoe's surface elevation, and the single channel every drop leaving it passes through. Daily mean outflow at the outlet has been measured since 1895 — 131 years, a baseline no new sensor network can manufacture.

Tributaries

USGS gauges on the creeks feeding the lake, monitored for discharge, sediment and water quality since the late 1980s. Five carry real-time turbidity — the same measure as TEON's in-lake sondes, from independent instruments, which makes them the cross-check on readings like Sunnyside's faulty turbidity channel.

Forest stations

Each station is a Campbell logger reading soil moisture and temperature at two depths, air temperature and humidity, and at three Glenbrook sites a set of eight band dendrometers measuring stem diameter — which both grows through the season and swells and shrinks daily with the tree's water content.

Field cameras

Five stations carry cameras logging into TEON's LoggerNet bucket.

Wildfire in the water

Smoke deposition delivers nutrients to the lake; nutrients feed algae; algae erode clarity. Overlaying NASA FIRMS active-fire detections and HRRR-Smoke plume forecasts on the chlorophyll and turbidity signals above lets us watch a fire's fingerprint arrive in the lake.
